ROCE: the complete guide

ROCE asks a powerful question: how effectively does a business turn the capital tied up in its operations into operating profit?

IN 30 SECONDSROCE connects operating profit with the capital required to produce it.

Use it across time and against relevant peers. Do not use one percentage as a standalone conclusion.

1. The formula

A common formulation is ROCE = EBIT ÷ Capital employed × 100.

EBIT is operating profit before interest and tax. Capital employed is commonly defined as total assets minus current liabilities, or equity plus long-term debt, depending on the methodology. Different databases can therefore report different ROCE values.

METHODOLOGY MATTERSNever compare two ROCE numbers blindly.

Check whether both use the same profit definition, capital base and period.

2. Worked example

Imagine a company with ₹120 crore of EBIT and ₹600 crore of capital employed. ROCE = ₹120 ÷ ₹600 × 100 = 20%.

Now imagine EBIT rises to ₹150 crore while capital employed rises to ₹1,000 crore. Profit increased, but ROCE falls to 15%. The company grew while becoming less efficient at converting capital into operating returns.

3. Why investors care

A business that can reinvest capital at attractive returns has more potential to compound value than a business that requires large amounts of capital for modest returns. ROCE is therefore especially useful when studying reinvestment economics.

4. Look at the trend, not a single year

Build a multi-year series. Ask whether ROCE is improving, stable or deteriorating, and identify the operational reason.

  • margin expansion can raise ROCE;
  • higher asset utilisation can raise ROCE;
  • working-capital discipline can raise ROCE;
  • large new capacity can temporarily lower ROCE before revenue catches up;
  • acquisitions can change the capital base and distort comparisons.

5. The operating drivers behind ROCE

At a conceptual level, ROCE improves when operating margins improve, when the business generates more sales from its asset base, or when less capital is tied up in working capital and fixed assets for each unit of revenue.

This is why ROCE should lead you back to the income statement and balance sheet rather than being treated as an isolated number.

6. Industry context matters

Capital-intensive businesses may naturally have different return profiles from asset-light businesses. Compare companies with similar economics. A 25% ROCE in one industry should not automatically be treated as equivalent to 25% in another.

7. ROCE vs ROE

ROE measures returns relative to shareholders' equity. ROCE focuses on operating returns relative to capital employed. ROE can be boosted by leverage, while ROCE is designed to look more directly at operating efficiency before financing effects.

Using both can reveal useful differences: a company may have high ROE but mediocre operating returns because debt is doing much of the work.

8. High ROCE can still be a bad investment

Quality and valuation are different questions. A company can generate excellent returns but trade at a price that already assumes years of strong performance. Conversely, a temporarily low ROCE can occur during a heavy investment phase.

9. What can distort ROCE?

  • asset revaluations;
  • acquisitions and purchase accounting;
  • large cash balances;
  • temporary working-capital movements;
  • cyclical profit peaks or troughs;
  • different accounting definitions across data providers.

10. Research workflow

  1. Record the reported ROCE and methodology.
  2. Build a multi-year trend.
  3. Check EBIT and margin trends.
  4. Inspect capital employed and its major components.
  5. Compare relevant peers consistently.
  6. Ask whether incremental capital is earning attractive returns.
  7. Connect the result to valuation and growth.

11. ROCE checklist

  • ☐ Definition verified
  • ☐ Multi-year trend reviewed
  • ☐ EBIT/margin drivers understood
  • ☐ Working capital examined
  • ☐ Asset intensity understood
  • ☐ Peer methodology matched
  • ☐ Incremental returns considered
  • ☐ Valuation considered separately

12. The deeper question

Do not stop at “ROCE is 22%.” Ask: How much capital can this business reinvest, at what return, for how long? That question connects profitability to compounding.
