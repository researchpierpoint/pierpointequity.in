PEG Ratio Explained

Understand the price/earnings-to-growth ratio, its calculation and why it should not be used mechanically.

PEG attempts to relate a P/E multiple to an earnings-growth rate. It can add context to growth stocks but depends heavily on the growth estimate.

Formula

A simplified PEG is P/E divided by expected EPS growth. Different conventions can use historical or forecast growth and percentages rather than decimals.

Why it can help

A high P/E may be more understandable when sustainable growth is also high. PEG provides a quick way to combine the two variables.

Limitations

Growth quality, duration, cyclicality, debt, margins and capital requirements are not captured by a single PEG number.

Best use

Treat PEG as a screening aid and then examine the underlying earnings assumptions and valuation history.

Deep research: what PEG is trying to solve

PEG relates P/E to an earnings-growth rate. It is intended to put valuation and growth on the same page, but it is highly sensitive to the growth estimate.

Simple example

A 30× P/E with 30% expected earnings growth produces a PEG of 1.0 using the common shorthand. Change the growth forecast to 20% and PEG becomes 1.5.

Why this can mislead

Growth forecasts may be uncertain, growth may not persist, and two companies with the same growth rate can have very different returns on capital and competitive durability.

Use a range, not false precision

Test PEG under multiple growth assumptions and compare those assumptions with historical performance and industry economics.

PEG should complement, not replace, valuation work

Combine it with P/E history, cash flow, ROCE/ROE, balance-sheet strength and the sustainability of growth.

PEG checklist

  • ☐ Growth period defined
  • ☐ Forecast source documented
  • ☐ Multiple scenarios tested
  • ☐ Returns on capital checked
  • ☐ Cash conversion checked
