STOCK RESEARCH GUIDE

How to analyse a stock

A repeatable framework for understanding a company before forming an investment view. The order matters less than checking each area and keeping evidence separate from interpretation.

1. Understand the business

Start with what the company sells, who buys it, how it makes money, where it operates and what could change demand. Read the annual report and recent company disclosures rather than relying only on summaries.

2. Check growth and profitability

Review multi-year revenue, operating profit and profit after tax. Look at margins and whether growth comes from volume, pricing, acquisitions, new capacity or accounting changes.

3. Examine returns on capital

ROCE and related return measures help connect operating profit with the capital required to generate it. Compare the metric across several years and with relevant peers.

4. Read the balance sheet

Check debt, cash, net debt, working capital and interest burden. A fast-growing company can still require substantial external capital, so growth should be considered alongside funding needs.

5. Follow cash flow

Compare operating cash flow with reported profit and inspect capital expenditure. Persistent gaps can require explanation, especially when working capital is expanding.

6. Analyse valuation

Use measures such as P/E, EV/EBITDA, price-to-book or free-cash-flow yield where appropriate. Compare the current valuation with the company's own history, peers and the assumptions required to justify it.

7. Check ownership and governance

Review promoter holding, pledges where disclosed, institutional ownership and material governance disclosures. Ownership data should be dated because it changes over time.

8. Build the risk list

Write down specific ways the thesis could fail: weaker demand, margin pressure, execution problems, regulation, competition, customer concentration, leverage or valuation compression.

9. Separate facts from forecasts

Label each statement as a sourced fact, calculation, interpretation, forecast or unknown. This makes it easier to detect when a conclusion is relying on an assumption rather than evidence.

10. Keep the research point-in-time

Record the date and source for every material number. Do not use later information to judge what was knowable at an earlier date.
