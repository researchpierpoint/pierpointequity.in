Debt-to-equity ratio explained

Debt-to-equity is a leverage measure. It helps describe how much debt a company carries relative to shareholders' equity, but the ratio needs context.

Formula

Debt-to-equity = Total debt ÷ Shareholders' equity

The exact definition of debt can vary. Check the methodology used before comparing figures from different sources.

Why leverage matters

Debt creates contractual obligations such as interest and principal repayment. When operating cash flow weakens, high leverage can increase financial pressure.

Debt is not automatically bad

Some businesses can use stable cash flows to support prudent leverage. Capital-intensive industries can also have structurally different debt levels from asset-light businesses.

Check net debt too

A company with substantial cash may have a different financial position from a company with the same gross debt and little cash. Net debt, interest coverage and cash generation add useful context.

Questions to ask

  • Is debt rising faster than operating earnings?
  • Can operating cash flow comfortably service interest and maturities?
  • Is new debt funding productive growth or covering weak cash generation?
  • How does leverage compare with the company's own history and peers?

Deep research: what debt-to-equity actually tells you

Debt-to-equity compares borrowing with shareholders' equity. It is a leverage indicator, not a complete solvency assessment.

Why leverage changes the picture

Debt can amplify returns when operating returns exceed the cost of borrowing. It can also magnify losses and liquidity pressure when earnings weaken.

Look beyond the ratio

Study interest coverage, debt maturity, fixed vs floating rates, currency exposure, cash generation and covenant requirements where disclosed.

Capital-intensive businesses

Some industries naturally operate with more debt. Compare leverage against relevant peers and the stability of their cash flows.

Worked example

₹300 crore of debt against ₹600 crore of equity produces a 0.5× debt-to-equity ratio. If equity later falls because of losses, the ratio can rise even without additional borrowing.

Debt checklist

  • ☐ Gross and net debt known
  • ☐ Maturity profile reviewed
  • ☐ Interest burden understood
  • ☐ Cash flow covers obligations
  • ☐ Leverage compared with peers
